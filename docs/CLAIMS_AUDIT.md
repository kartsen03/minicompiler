## Claims to remove or reword

Remove: **T4 or Colab numbers** until the notebook has been run there.

Reword:

- **Claim 5.** "Reduce computation overhead" is true, but the effect depends
  on the graph. A defensible phrasing: *fusion and folding cut compute nodes
  22 → 4 on an MLP block and made elementwise-heavy graphs 2.3–2.9x faster on
  CPU and 10x faster on GPU.* Avoid implying a large end-to-end gain on
  matmul-heavy models (1.00–1.11x measured on CPU).
- **Claim 6 and 15.** "Benchmarked against PyTorch" is true. "Faster than
  PyTorch" needs qualifying. On the CPU the GELU win over PyTorch's fused
  kernels comes mostly from Eigen's cheaper, less accurate `tanh`, and PyTorch
  wins matmul-heavy graphs. On the GPU the fused kernel only matches PyTorch's
  own fused `F.gelu` at large sizes, and the lead over `torch.compile` there is
  host overhead rather than a better kernel; minicompiler wins the B=128 MLP
  block (1.86x over idiomatic PyTorch, from split-K) and PyTorch wins the
  larger matmul-heavy graphs by 3–12%. Say what was compared and where each
  side wins.
- **Claim 10.** Profiling used `perf` with a software timer event because
  WSL2 has no hardware counters. Don't claim cache-miss or IPC analysis.
- **Claim 13.** Say "x% of cuBLAS" with the shape, not "competitive with" or
  "beats" cuBLAS: the default kernel reaches 75–104% of cuBLAS on square and
  odd shapes (93% at 2048³) and 89–145% on the MLP-layer shapes, the 145% on
  one small-output layer (128×2048×512) where split-K suits the shape better
  than cuBLAS's kernel.
- **Claim 18.** Say "63–110% of cuBLAS's tensor-core GEMM in the same input
  format" and "the FP16 kernel is 1.5–2.2x faster than the FP32 default, at
  300 times its error". Don't claim a TF32 speedup on this GPU: Nsight Compute reports
  the same peak for TF32 tensor cores as for FP32 (256 FLOP per SM per clock),
  and the measured gain is 1.02–1.21x. The kernels use WMMA, not `mma.sync`
  with `cp.async`; say so if asked how they differ from cuBLAS's CUTLASS
  kernels.

## How the numbers were measured

The CPU numbers come from an i7-12700H laptop under WSL2 with the Windows
"Silent" power plan. A pinned core's speed there drifts by up to 2x within a
minute, so every comparison is timed in interleaved rounds within one process,
reported as the median of per-round ratios, and repeated in three runs; the
result files keep every raw run. An earlier PyTorch comparison that timed
minicompiler in separate processes was discarded because of this drift.

The GPU numbers come from the same laptop's RTX 3060 Laptop GPU with the
"Turbo" plan (under "Silent" its SM clock is held near 900 MHz). The GPU idles
at 210 MHz, needs about half a second of sustained load to boost to 2 GHz,
and sags as it heats, so each benchmark warms up for a second, times its
variants interleaved, samples the SM clock every round, and runs three times;
every table records the clock range, the commit and the CUDA versions.
Elementwise kernels are timed with CUDA events around each launch; matmul
kernels between event nodes captured with them into a CUDA graph, so that
host-side launch overhead, which differs between cuBLAS and these kernels, is
left out for all of them. Peak bandwidth and FP32 throughput are computed
from the device's properties at run time; tensor-core peaks are the ones
Nsight Compute reports for the GPU.
